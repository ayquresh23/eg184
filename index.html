Stress & Strain

The tensile test is the foundation of mechanical testing. Before you can understand any other topic in this module you need to be able to read a stress–strain curve and know what every feature on it means.

In this topic
  • → The tensile test
  • → Engineering stress & strain
  • → Features of the stress–strain curve
  • → Young's modulus & Poisson's ratio
  • → Hardness

The Tensile Test

A cylindrical specimen with a narrow gauge section is pulled at constant displacement rate. A load cell measures force; an extensometer measures gauge-length change. From these two raw signals we derive engineering stress and strain — quantities that describe the material, not the geometry.

Why stress & strain, not force & displacement?

A thicker bar carries more load before yielding — but the material's yield strength is the same. Dividing by area (→ stress) and original length (→ strain) removes the geometry and gives properties of the material itself. That's why a stress–strain curve can be used across different specimen sizes.

Engineering Stress & Strain

Engineering stress
$$\sigma = \frac{F}{A_0}$$
Fapplied force (N)
A₀original cross-sectional area (m² or mm²)
σengineering stress (Pa or MPa)
Engineering strain
$$\varepsilon = \frac{l_i - l_0}{l_0}$$
l₀original gauge length
lᵢinstantaneous length under load
εengineering strain (dimensionless)

Features of the Stress–Strain Curve

Elastic region (linear)

Slope = Young's modulus E. Deformation fully recoverable. Governed by Hooke's Law: σ = Eε.

Yield point / 0.2% proof stress

Start of permanent deformation. For materials without a sharp yield point, use the 0.2% offset — draw a line parallel to the elastic region from ε = 0.002.

Strain hardening region

Between yield and UTS. Dislocation density increases — material gets stronger as it deforms. Slope decreases toward UTS.

UTS (peak)

Maximum engineering stress. Onset of necking. Engineering stress drops after this because A decreases faster than F.

Fracture point

Final separation. Engineering stress at fracture < UTS due to necking. True stress at fracture is still rising.

Worked example — reading a stress–strain curve

Cylindrical specimen: gauge length 65 mm, diameter 10 mm. Test data gives UTS = 572 MPa, the 0.2% line intersects the curve at 265 MPa, and the specimen fractures at ε = 0.30 with elastic springback ε_e = 0.04.

A₀ = π(0.01)²/4 = 7.85 × 10⁻⁵ m²
0.2% proof stress = 265 MPa (where ε = 0.002 offset line meets curve)
UTS = 572 MPa (peak of curve)
Plastic strain to failure = total strain − elastic springback = 0.30 − 0.04 = 0.26
E = slope of elastic region = σ/ε in the linear zone. Toughness = area under entire curve (J/m³).

Young's Modulus & Poisson's Ratio

Hooke's Law
$$\sigma = E\varepsilon \quad \text{(elastic regime only)}$$
EYoung's (elastic) modulus (GPa) — slope of linear elastic region
Poisson's ratio
$$\nu = -\frac{\varepsilon_x}{\varepsilon_z} \approx 0.3 \text{ for metals}$$
ε_xlateral (transverse) strain — compressive under tensile axial load
ε_zaxial strain — along load direction
Worked example — Poisson's ratio

Cylinder: d₀ = 10 mm. Under tensile load, diameter decreases by Δd = −2.5×10⁻³ mm. ν = 0.3. Find axial strain.

ε_x = Δd/d₀ = −2.5×10⁻³/10 = −2.5×10⁻⁴
ε_z = −ε_x/ν = −(−2.5×10⁻⁴)/0.3
ε_z = 8.3×10⁻⁴ (tensile, as expected)

Hardness

Hardness measures resistance to localised plastic deformation (penetration). The Vickers test uses a square-based pyramidal diamond indenter (136° included angle). The diagonal of the resulting impression is measured.

Hardness rule of thumb
$$\sigma_y \approx \frac{H}{3}$$

Hardness H in MPa, yield strength σ_y in MPa. Useful for quick estimates where a full tensile test is not possible.

Deformation Processes in Crystals

Metals are crystalline. Their plastic deformation doesn't happen by simultaneous shear of entire planes — it happens by slip, driven by dislocations moving along specific planes in specific directions. This topic explains why, and gives you the tools to calculate when slip starts.

In this topic
  • → Crystal structures & slip systems
  • → Resolved shear stress & Schmid's Law
  • → Theoretical vs experimental strength

Crystal Structures & Slip Systems

StructureSlip planeSlip directionNo. of systemsDuctility
FCC (Al, Cu, Ni, Au){111}⟨110⟩12 (4 planes × 3 dir.)High
BCC (Fe, W, Mo){110}⟨111⟩12Medium
HCP (Ti, Mg, Zn){001}⟨100⟩3 primaryLow
Why are slip planes and directions the densest ones?

Densely packed planes have the largest inter-planar spacing → weakest bonding between planes → easiest to shear. Densely packed directions have the shortest repeat distance → smallest Burgers vector → lowest energy for dislocation motion.

Resolved Shear Stress & Schmid's Law

Even if you pull a crystal in pure tension, the force creates a shear component on inclined slip planes. That shear component is what actually drives slip.

Resolved shear stress (3D Schmid)
$$\tau_{RSS} = \sigma \cos\lambda \cos\phi = \sigma m$$
λangle between tensile axis and slip DIRECTION
φangle between tensile axis and slip plane NORMAL
mSchmid factor = cosλ cosφ. Maximum 0.5 at λ = φ = 45°
Yield stress from Schmid's Law
$$\sigma_y = \frac{\tau_{CRSS}}{\cos\lambda \cos\phi} = \frac{\tau_{CRSS}}{m}$$

Slip initiates on the system with the highest Schmid factor m (primary slip system). Slip starts when τ_RSS reaches τ_CRSS.

Derivation — resolved shear stress from first principles

Force F applied axially to a crystal. Find the shear stress on a slip plane whose normal makes angle φ with the tensile axis, in a slip direction making angle λ with the tensile axis.

Component of force along slip direction: F cosλ
Area of the slip plane: A₀/cosφ (the inclined area is larger than the cross-section)
τ_RSS = (F cosλ)/(A₀/cosφ) = (F/A₀) cosλ cosφ
τ_RSS = σ cosλ cosφ = σm. Yield when τ_RSS = τ_CRSS → σ_y = τ_CRSS/m

Theoretical vs Experimental Strength

If plastic deformation required all atoms in a plane to move simultaneously, the required stress would be enormous:

Theoretical maximum shear stress
$$\tau_{max} \approx \frac{G}{2\pi}$$

This gives ~3–30 GPa for metals. Experimental CRSS is only 0.5–10 MPa — a discrepancy of 10³–10⁴×. This proves that dislocations must exist and that they enable slip at much lower stresses.

MetalG/2π (GPa)Experimental CRSS (MPa)Ratio
Al11.30.78~10⁴
Cu200.5~4×10⁴
Ti1714~10³
Ni325~6×10³

EXAM CHEAT SHEET
Quick recap
  • FCC slip: {111}⟨110⟩, 12 systems; BCC: {110}⟨111⟩, 12 systems; HCP: 3 primary systems
  • RSS = σ cosλ cosφ = σm (λ to slip direction; φ to slip plane NORMAL)
  • Schmid factor max = 0.5 (not 1.0). τ_max = σ/2
  • Slip when τ_RSS ≥ τ_CRSS. Primary slip system = highest m
  • φ is to plane NORMAL, not the plane itself — most common exam mix-up
  • Theoretical G/2π >> experimental CRSS by 10³–10⁴×: proof dislocations exist
  • RSS = 0 when either λ = 90° (force ⊥ slip direction) or φ = 90° (force in slip plane)

Dislocations & Work Hardening

Dislocations are the key to understanding why metals are ductile, why cold working strengthens them, and why grain boundaries resist slip. Everything in this topic comes back to one idea: moving a single row of atoms at a time takes far less energy than moving a whole plane simultaneously.

In this topic
  • → Four dislocation types
  • → Burgers vector
  • → Frank-Read sources
  • → Dislocation motion: cross-slip & climb
  • → Work hardening & Hall-Petch

Four Dislocation Types

Edge dislocation

b ⊥ dislocation line. Extra half-plane of atoms inserted. Moves parallel to b under shear stress. Symbol: ⊥ (positive), ⊤ (negative).

Screw dislocation

b ∥ dislocation line. No extra half-plane; atomic planes spiral helically. Moves perpendicular to shear stress direction. Can cross-slip. Symbol: ⊙ (RHS), ⊗ (LHS).

Mixed dislocation

b inclined to line. Resolves into edge component b_e = b sinθ and screw component b_s = b cosθ. Most dislocations in real crystals are mixed.

Dislocation loop

Closed loop with constant b. Opposite sides have opposite polarity (one edge +, one edge −). Cannot end inside a crystal — must close on itself, reach a surface, or end at a node.

Burgers Vector

The Burgers vector b defines the direction and magnitude of atomic displacement associated with a dislocation. Determined by a Burgers circuit: draw a closed loop around the dislocation using the right-hand screw (RHS) convention. The closure failure vector is b.

Strain energy (per unit length)
$$U_{screw} = Gb^2 \quad;\quad U_{edge} = \frac{Gb^2}{1-\nu}$$

Edge dislocations have higher energy than screw by factor 1/(1−ν). Dislocation loops are therefore elliptical, not circular.

Force per unit length on a dislocation
$$F = \tau b$$

Frank-Read Sources

How does dislocation density increase from ~10¹⁰ to ~10¹⁶ m⁻² during cold work? Frank-Read sources are the answer — they continuously regenerate new dislocation loops from a single pinned segment.

Frank-Read mechanism
  1. A dislocation segment is pinned at two points (by impurities, nodes, or forest dislocations), length l
  2. Applied shear stress causes it to bow outward
  3. It expands, wraps around, and the two arms meet and annihilate (opposite polarity)
  4. A complete dislocation loop is emitted; the original segment reforms
  5. Process repeats — one source can produce thousands of loops
Frank-Read critical stress
$$\tau_{FR} = \frac{Gb}{l}$$
llength of the PINNED SEGMENT (not grain diameter)
Gshear modulus; bBurgers vector magnitude

Dislocation Motion: Cross-Slip & Climb

Cross-slip (screw dislocations only)

A screw dislocation changes from one slip plane to another that also contains its Burgers vector. Allows bypass of obstacles. Cannot happen for edge dislocations — their line and b together uniquely define one slip plane.

Dislocation climb (edge dislocations only, T > 0.4T_m)

An edge dislocation moves perpendicular to its slip plane by absorbing or emitting vacancies. Non-conservative (changes dislocation density). Thermally activated — requires T > 0.4T_m. Enables creep.

Work Hardening & Hall-Petch

As deformation proceeds, dislocation density ρ rises from ~10¹⁰ to ~10¹⁶ m⁻². More dislocations = more obstacles for other dislocations = higher stress needed to continue deformation = work hardening.

Hall-Petch equation
$$\sigma_y = \sigma_0 + k_y \cdot d^{-1/2}$$
σ₀lattice friction stress (MPa)
k_yHall-Petch coefficient (MPa·mm^(−½) or MPa·m^(−½))
dmean grain diameter (must be consistent units with k_y)

Grain boundaries block dislocation motion — dislocations pile up. Finer grains → more boundaries → higher yield stress. Breaks down below ~10 nm (grain boundary sliding takes over).

Worked example — Hall-Petch for 70/30 Brass

σ₀ = 25 MPa, k_y = 12.5 MPa·mm^(−½). Find σ_y for d = 500 μm and d = 5 μm.

d = 500 μm = 0.5 mm: σ_y = 25 + 12.5 × (0.5)^(−½) = 25 + 17.7 = 42.7 MPa
d = 5 μm = 0.005 mm: σ_y = 25 + 12.5 × (0.005)^(−½) = 25 + 176.8 = 201.8 MPa
100× finer grain → 5× higher yield strength. Always check units: d and k_y must match (both mm or both m).

Topic 3 essentials

EXAM CHEAT SHEET
Quick recap
  • Edge: b ⊥ line; Screw: b ∥ line; Mixed: b inclined; Loop: closed, constant b
  • Cross-slip: ONLY screw. Climb: ONLY edge, T > 0.4T_m, non-conservative
  • Frank-Read critical stress: τ = Gb/l (l = pinned segment length, NOT grain size)
  • Hall-Petch: σ_y = σ₀ + k_y·d^(−½). Units of d and k_y must match
  • Dislocations cannot end inside crystal: must loop, reach surface, or end at node
  • At dislocation node: Σbᵢ = 0
  • Dislocation density: annealed ~10¹⁰ m⁻²; cold-worked ~10¹⁶ m⁻²
  • Edge energy > screw energy by factor 1/(1−ν)

Strengthening Mechanisms & Annealing

There are four main ways to strengthen a metal: solid solution, work hardening, grain refinement (Hall-Petch, covered in T3), and precipitation hardening. Each one works by impeding dislocation motion. Annealing does the reverse — it removes the effects of cold work.

In this topic
  • → Solid solution strengthening
  • → Precipitation / age hardening
  • → Cold work & annealing

Solid Solution Strengthening

Dissolving a solute atom in a host lattice distorts the crystal locally. This strain field interacts with passing dislocations and impedes their motion, raising the yield stress.

Solid solution strengthening increment
$$\Delta\sigma_s = k_s \cdot c^{1/2}$$
csolute atomic fraction
k_smaterial constant (depends on misfit strain)

Scales as c^(1/2), NOT linearly. Interstitials (e.g. C in Fe) cause both dilation AND shear strain → stronger effect than substitutionals of similar misfit.

Cottrell atmospheres in steel

Carbon interstitials migrate to the tensile stress field of edge dislocations, forming a "cloud" (Cottrell atmosphere). This pins the dislocation. To start plastic flow, you must first rip the dislocation free — this produces the sharp upper yield point. After detachment, flow continues at the lower (true) yield stress. This is why mild steel shows a sharp yield drop.

Precipitation / Age Hardening

The most powerful strengthening mechanism. Requires a phase diagram with a solvus — solubility must decrease with decreasing temperature.

Three-step heat treatment
  1. Solution treat — heat into single-phase region; dissolve all solute
  2. Quench — rapid cool to room temperature; retain supersaturated solid solution
  3. Age — hold below solvus; controlled nucleation and growth of fine precipitates
Orowan critical shear stress (incoherent precipitates)
$$\tau_y \approx \frac{Gb}{L}$$
Linter-particle spacing (mean free path)
Gshear modulus; bBurgers vector

τ_y ∝ 1/L: wider spacing = lower critical stress. This explains over-aging — as particles coarsen, L increases, and strength falls.

Aging statePrecipitateDislocation interactionStrength
Under-agedSmall, coherentCutting (shear through)Below peak
Peak-agedOptimal sizeMix of cutting and loopingMaximum
Over-agedLarge, incoherent (coarsened)Orowan looping (bypass)Falls

Cold Work & Annealing

Cold work = plastic deformation below 0.4T_m. Effects: grain elongation, dislocation density 10⁶→10¹² /cm³, increased strength, decreased ductility, increased electrical resistance.

Three stages of annealing
StageWhat happensEffect on strengthEffect on ductility
1. RecoveryDislocations rearrange to low-energy sub-grain walls (polygonisation). Residual stress relievedLittle changeLittle change
2. RecrystallisationNew strain-free equiaxed grains nucleate at grain boundaries and grow. Dislocation density drops dramaticallyLarge decreaseLarge increase
3. Grain growthLarge grains consume small ones. Total grain boundary area minimisedSlight decreaseSlight decrease
Key exam distinction

Recovery barely changes mechanical properties — it mainly restores conductivity. Recrystallisation causes the largest changes to strength and ductility. Recrystallisation temperature decreases with more prior cold work (more stored energy = easier nucleation).

EXAM CHEAT SHEET
Quick recap
  • Solid solution strengthening: Δσ ∝ c^(1/2), not linear in c
  • Precipitation hardening: solution treat → quench → age
  • Orowan stress: τ ≈ Gb/L. Over-aging → L increases → strength falls
  • Peak aged = maximum hardness. Over-aged = coarsened particles, Orowan bypass, lower strength
  • Recovery: restores conductivity, barely changes mechanical properties
  • Recrystallisation: biggest mechanical property change (strength ↓↓, ductility ↑↑)
  • Cold work boundary: T < 0.4T_m in KELVIN
  • Dispersion-strengthened materials retain strength at high T; precipitation-hardened do not

Fracture & Fracture Mechanics

Fracture is the most catastrophic form of mechanical failure. This topic gives you the tools to quantify it — from reading an SEM image to calculating the maximum safe stress for a component with a known crack.

In this topic
  • → Ductile vs brittle fracture
  • → Ductile-to-brittle transition
  • → Griffith criterion
  • → Stress intensity factor & K_IC

Ductile vs Brittle Fracture

Ductile fracture
  • Large plastic deformation before fracture
  • Slow, stable crack growth — needs increasing stress
  • Strain-controlled, high energy absorbed
  • Cup-and-cone morphology in tension
  • Dimpled (dimple = microvoid) surface in SEM
  • Preferred: visible warning (necking) before failure
Brittle fracture
  • Little or no plastic deformation
  • Rapid, unstable crack propagation
  • Stress-controlled, low energy absorbed
  • Flat, granular, shiny surface
  • Cleavage facets or intergranular cracking in SEM
  • Dangerous: no warning — sudden catastrophic failure

Ductile-to-Brittle Transition (DBT)

BCC and HCP metals show a transition from ductile to brittle behaviour as temperature decreases. FCC metals do NOT show this transition (Al, Cu, austenitic stainless steel are safe at all temperatures).

Charpy impact test

Notched bar struck by pendulum. Energy absorbed = mg(h_start − h_end). Plots impact energy vs temperature, showing the transition. QUALITATIVE only — cannot be used directly in design calculations. Transition temperature T_t = temperature at 50% of upper shelf energy. Finer grain → lower T_t (better). More impurities → higher T_t (worse).

Griffith Criterion (Perfectly Brittle Materials)

Griffith critical fracture stress
$$\sigma_f = \sqrt{\frac{2E\gamma}{\pi a}}$$
γsurface energy (J/m²) — energy to create new surface per unit area
ahalf crack length for internal crack; full crack length for edge crack
EYoung's modulus (Pa)

For perfectly brittle materials only (glass, ceramics). For metals and polymers, use Orowan-Irwin modification with effective surface energy γ_e = γ_s + γ_p where γ_p (plastic work) >> γ_s.

Stress Intensity Factor & Fracture Toughness

Stress intensity factor (Mode I)
$$K = \alpha\sigma\sqrt{\pi a}$$
αgeometry factor (= 1 for edge crack in infinite plate)
acrack length (edge crack convention)
Kstress intensity factor (MPa√m)
Fracture condition & safe stress
$$K \geq K_{IC} \Rightarrow \text{unstable fracture}$$
$$\sigma_f = \frac{K_{IC}}{\alpha\sqrt{\pi a}}$$
K_ICfracture toughness — material property (MPa√m). Pure metals: up to 200; Mild steel: ~50; Al alloys: ~20–40; Glass: ~0.2–1
Relationship between G_c and K_IC
$$K_{IC}^2 = EG_C$$
G_Ccritical strain energy release rate (J/m²) — energy released per unit new crack area
Case study — De Havilland Comet (1954)

Early pressurised aircraft. Square windows with sharp corners created stress concentrations. Fatigue cracks grew from rivet holes. G_c ≈ 300 psi·in; applied stress ≈ 20,000 psi ≈ 138 MPa.

a_c = G_c/(πσ²) = (300 × 0.0254) / (π × 138²×10⁶) ≈ 67 mm = 2.6 inches
When the crack reached the window edge, the window width (~20 inches) was added to the crack length
Total effective crack length instantly exceeded a_c → catastrophic explosive decompression. Modern aircraft use rounded windows to eliminate stress concentrations.

EXAM CHEAT SHEET
Quick recap
  • Ductile: strain-controlled, high energy, cup-and-cone, dimples in SEM
  • Brittle: stress-controlled, low energy, flat cleavage, no warning
  • DBT: BCC and HCP show it; FCC does NOT
  • Charpy: qualitative only. T_t = 50% upper shelf energy. Fine grain → lower T_t
  • Griffith: brittle only. σ_f = √(2Eγ/πa). Internal crack: a = half-length; edge crack: a = full length
  • K = σ√(πa) (α = 1 for edge crack). Fracture when K ≥ K_IC
  • K units: MPa√m; G units: J/m². K_IC² = EG_c
  • σ_f ∝ 1/√a — doubling crack length reduces safe stress by √2

Fatigue

Fatigue is the most common cause of engineering fracture — responsible for 50–90% of all mechanical failures. A part can fail under cyclic loads far below its UTS, often with no visible deformation. The surface is everything in fatigue.

In this topic
  • → Stress parameters & S-N curves
  • → Stage I & Stage II crack growth
  • → Striations vs beachmarks
  • → Paris Law
  • → Improving fatigue performance

Stress Parameters & S-N Curves

Cyclic stress parameters
$$R = \frac{\sigma_{min}}{\sigma_{max}} \qquad \Delta\sigma = \sigma_{max} - \sigma_{min} \qquad \sigma_{amp} = \frac{\Delta\sigma}{2} \qquad \sigma_m = \frac{\sigma_{max}+\sigma_{min}}{2}$$
R = −1fully reversed (equal tension & compression, σ_m = 0)
R = 0zero-to-max (pulsating tension)
R = 0.5high mean stress (σ_m = 0.75σ_max)
S-N curve (Wöhler diagram)

Stress amplitude S plotted against log N_f (cycles to failure). For ferritic steels: curve flattens at a true fatigue limit (~UTS/2) below which infinite life is predicted. For Al, Ti, non-ferrous: no true limit — the curve keeps falling. Endurance quoted at 10⁶–10⁷ cycles instead.

Stage I & Stage II Crack Growth

Stage I — Initiation
  • Crack grows at 45° to tensile axis along crystallographic slip planes
  • Microstructure-dependent; crack size < one grain diameter
  • Dominated by N_i for smooth specimens
  • Driven by persistent slip bands (PSBs) — intrusions nucleate cracks
Stage II — Propagation
  • Crack grows perpendicular to tensile axis
  • Less microstructure-dependent
  • Governed by Paris Law (da/dN = C(ΔK)^m)
  • Blunting-resharpening mechanism: crack tip blunts on tensile half-cycle, resharpens on compressive

Striations vs Beachmarks

Striations

  • Microscopic — visible only by SEM
  • One per cycle
  • Stage II mechanism
  • Absent in fast-fracture (Stage III) zone

Beachmarks (clamshell marks)

  • Macroscopic — visible to naked eye
  • NOT one cycle each — each mark = thousands of striations
  • Caused by loading interruptions (machine stop, temperature change)
  • Concentric around initiation site

Paris Law

Paris Law (Stage II crack growth)
$$\frac{da}{dN} = C(\Delta K)^m \qquad \Delta K = Y\Delta\sigma\sqrt{\pi a}$$
da/dNcrack growth rate per cycle (m/cycle)
ΔKstress intensity factor range = K_max − K_min
C, mmaterial constants. For metals: m ≈ 2–4

Valid for Stage II only. There is a threshold ΔK_th below which cracks do not grow (very long life). Above K_IC, fast fracture occurs.

Improving Fatigue Performance

Surface finish

Smooth polished surfaces extend fatigue life — surface notches act as stress concentrators for Stage I crack initiation. Always machine perpendicular to expected load direction.

Shot peening

Creates a compressive residual stress layer ~¼–½ shot diameter deep. Compressive stress opposes crack opening → dramatically increases N_i. Widely used in aerospace (turbine blades, landing gear). Over-peening is counterproductive — introduces subsurface tensile stress.

Case hardening

Carburising or nitriding introduces hard, strong, compressive surface layer. Raises threshold for crack initiation. Used in gears and shafts.

Mean stress correction — Goodman (conservative) & Gerber
$$\text{Goodman: } \sigma_a = \sigma_e\left[1 - \frac{\sigma_m}{\sigma_u}\right] \qquad \text{Gerber: } \sigma_a = \sigma_e\left[1 - \left(\frac{\sigma_m}{\sigma_u}\right)^2\right]$$

Tensile mean stress reduces fatigue life. Use Goodman for design (conservative). Gerber is less conservative and fits experimental data better but is not used for safety-critical work.

Topic 6 essentials

EXAM CHEAT SHEET
Quick recap
  • Fatigue occurs BELOW UTS — often below yield. Never use static strength for cyclic design
  • R = σ_min/σ_max. R = −1: fully reversed. R = 0: pulsating. R = 0.5: high mean stress
  • Stage I: 45° to stress axis. Stage II: perpendicular. Stage III: fast fracture
  • Striations: one per cycle, SEM only. Beachmarks: macroscopic, NOT one cycle each
  • Paris Law Stage II only: da/dN = C(ΔK)^m
  • Ferritic steels: true fatigue limit ~UTS/2. Al, Ti: no true limit
  • Shot peening: compressive residual stress → opposes crack opening
  • Tensile mean stress reduces life; compressive extends it
  • N_f = N_i + N_p. Smooth specimen: N_i dominant. Pre-cracked: N_p dominant

Creep & Stress Corrosion Cracking

Creep is time-dependent deformation under constant stress at elevated temperature. SCC is cracking driven by the simultaneous action of stress, environment, and a susceptible material. Both are insidious because they cause failure below the yield stress.

In this topic
  • → Three creep stages
  • → Power Law & Arrhenius
  • → Creep mechanisms
  • → Stress Corrosion Cracking

Three Creep Stages

When does creep matter?

Creep is significant when T > 0.3–0.4T_m in KELVIN. Lead (T_m = 600 K) creeps under its own weight at room temperature (300 K = 0.5T_m). Copper pipes (T_m = 1356 K): at room temperature, T/T_m = 0.22 → no creep. Always compute T/T_m in Kelvin, never Celsius.

Power Law & Arrhenius

Norton's Power Law (stress dependence)
$$\dot{\varepsilon}_s \propto \sigma^n \quad;\quad \log\dot{\varepsilon}_s = \log A + n\log\sigma$$
nstress exponent. n ≈ 1: diffusional creep. n ≈ 5: dislocation creep (most engineering situations)
Arrhenius (temperature dependence)
$$\dot{\varepsilon}_s \propto \exp\!\left(-\frac{Q_C}{RT}\right) \quad;\quad \ln\dot{\varepsilon}_s = \text{const} - \frac{Q_C}{R}\cdot\frac{1}{T}$$
Q_Cactivation energy for creep (J/mol)
R8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹
TALWAYS in KELVIN

Q_C from experiment: plot ln ε̇_s vs 1/T. Gradient = −Q_C/R. Q_C = −R × gradient (the minus sign is critical and frequently forgotten).

Combined Power Law (full equation)
$$\dot{\varepsilon}_s = A\sigma^n \exp\!\left(-\frac{Q_C}{RT}\right)$$

When A is unknown: use ratios for same material, different conditions — A cancels.

Worked example — determining Q_C from Arrhenius plot

At T₁ = 900 K: ε̇_s = 2.0×10⁻⁵ s⁻¹. At T₂ = 800 K: ε̇_s = 8.0×10⁻⁷ s⁻¹. Find Q_C.

ln(2.0×10⁻⁵) = −10.82 at 1/T₁ = 1.111×10⁻³ K⁻¹
ln(8.0×10⁻⁷) = −14.04 at 1/T₂ = 1.250×10⁻³ K⁻¹
Gradient = (−10.82 − (−14.04)) / (1.111×10⁻³ − 1.250×10⁻³) = 3.22 / (−1.39×10⁻⁴) = −23,165 K
Q_C = −R × gradient = −8.314 × (−23,165) = 193 kJ/mol

Why diffusional creep rates don't match experiments

Calculated Nabarro-Herring and Coble rates at engineering stresses are 6–7 orders of magnitude below observed rates. Dislocation creep (n ≈ 5) dominates in practice. The diffusional mechanisms only matter at very high temperatures and very low stresses.

Stress Corrosion Cracking (SCC)

Three simultaneous conditions required — remove any one to stop SCC
  1. Tensile stress (applied or residual, e.g. from welding)
  2. Susceptible material (austenitic SS in Cl⁻; brass in ammonia; high-strength steel in H₂)
  3. Reactive environment (Cl⁻ ions, H₂S, ammonia, etc.)

Pitting corrosion → SCC initiation

Cl⁻ breaks down passive oxide film locally. Inside pit: stagnant electrolyte, O₂ depleted, Cl⁻ enriched, pH ≈ 2. Pit acts as a sharp stress concentrator → SCC crack initiates from pit base.

Hydrogen embrittlement

Atomic H (radius 53 pm) diffuses into lattice, accumulates at stress concentrations and grain boundaries. BCC and HCP most susceptible. As little as 0.0001 wt% H can crack high-strength steels. Worst at intermediate temperatures and low strain rates.

Prevention strategies
  • Material selection — avoid susceptible alloy-environment combinations
  • Remove the environment — deaerate, reduce Cl⁻ concentration, use inhibitors
  • Reduce stress — post-weld heat treatment to relieve residual stresses; design to avoid stress concentrations
  • Cathodic protection — make the structure the cathode (galvanic or impressed current)
  • Protective coatings — prevent environmental access to metal surface

EXAM CHEAT SHEET
Quick recap
  • Creep significant when T > 0.3–0.4T_m in KELVIN. Lead creeps at room temperature
  • Secondary (steady-state) creep rate ε̇_s is the design-critical parameter
  • Power Law: ε̇_s ∝ σ^n. n ≈ 1 → diffusional; n ≈ 5 → dislocation creep
  • Arrhenius: ln ε̇_s vs 1/T → gradient = −Q_C/R → Q_C = −R × gradient (mind the sign!)
  • Combined: ε̇_s = Aσ^n exp(−Q_C/RT). Use ratios when A is unknown
  • Nabarro-Herring: ε̇ ∝ σ/d². Coble: ε̇ ∝ σ/d³. Both n ≈ 1
  • SCC needs ALL THREE: tensile stress + reactive environment + susceptible material
  • SCC causes brittle failure of normally DUCTILE metals — its defining danger
  • Hydrogen embrittlement worst near room temperature and at LOW strain rates

Determining Q_C from two temperature data points

Same alloy, same stress: at 700°C (973 K) ε̇_s = 3.0×10⁻⁵ s⁻¹; at 600°C (873 K) ε̇_s = 6.0×10⁻⁷ s⁻¹. Find Q_C.

ln(3.0×10⁻⁵) = −10.41; 1/973 = 1.028×10⁻³ K⁻¹
ln(6.0×10⁻⁷) = −14.33; 1/873 = 1.146×10⁻³ K⁻¹

(b) ε_z = 0.1/50 = 0.002

Find: (a) yield strength; (b) elastic springback at fracture; (c) plastic strain to failure; (d) which quantity is larger — yield strength or 0.2% proof stress?
(a) Yield strength = 300 MPa (where linearity ends)
(b) Elastic springback = σ_fracture/E = 250×10⁶/200×10⁹ = 0.00125
(c) Plastic strain = 0.35 − 0.00125 = 0.349

Dislocations & Deformation (T2, T3)

T2 — Schmid's Law
A single crystal of copper (τ_CRSS = 0.5 MPa) is loaded in tension. The primary slip system has λ = 35° and φ = 60°. Find: (a) the Schmid factor; (b) the tensile yield stress; (c) the resolved shear stress at σ = 1.5 MPa.

An aluminium crystal (G = 26 GPa, b = 0.286 nm) has a Frank-Read source with pinned segment length l = 1.5 μm. (a) What shear stress is needed to activate this source? (b) If grain size d = 20 μm with one source per grain, how does τ_FR compare to the Hall-Petch strengthening increment (assume k_y = 0.07 MPa·m^(½), σ₀ = 10 MPa)?

T5 — K_IC and safe stress
A steel plate (K_IC = 55 MPa√m) contains an edge crack of length 3 mm. (a) What is the maximum safe applied stress (α = 1)? (b) If the applied stress is 300 MPa, what is the maximum safe crack length?

Fatigue (T6)

T6 — Goodman correction
A steel component (UTS = 800 MPa, fatigue limit σ_e = 400 MPa at R = −1) operates with a mean stress σ_m = 200 MPa. Using the Goodman criterion, find the allowable stress amplitude. Compare to the uncorrected fatigue limit.

Creep (T7)

T7 — Power Law creep ratio
A nickel superalloy follows ε̇_s = Aσ^n exp(−Q_C/RT) with n = 5 and Q_C = 280 kJ/mol. At 900°C (1173 K) and σ = 100 MPa, ε̇_s = 1.2×10⁻⁸ s⁻¹. Find the creep rate at (a) 950°C (1223 K), same stress; (b) 900°C, σ = 150 MPa.
(a) ε̇₂/ε̇₁ = exp(−Q_C/RT₂)/exp(−Q_C/RT₁) = exp[Q_C/R × (1/T₁ − 1/T₂)]

MCQ Traps

Plausible-but-wrong claims the exam uses. Read each one. Know what's actually true.

Topic 1 — Stress & Strain

T1 — T1
"UTS is the stress at which the material fractures."
FALSE. UTS is the maximum engineering stress on the curve — the onset of necking. After UTS, engineering stress drops because A decreases faster than F, but the material has not fractured yet. Fracture stress (engineering) is lower than UTS.
T1 — T2
"The 0.2% proof stress is found by offsetting ε = 0.2 on the strain axis."
FALSE. The offset is ε = 0.002 (not 0.2). 0.2% strain = 0.002 in decimal form. Draw the line parallel to the elastic slope starting from ε = 0.002.
T1 — T3
"Stiffness and toughness are the same thing."
FALSE. Stiffness = Young's modulus E (slope of elastic region). Toughness = energy absorbed before fracture (area under the full stress–strain curve, J/m³). A stiff material is not necessarily tough.
T1 — T4
"Plastic strain to failure equals total strain to failure."
FALSE. Plastic strain = total strain − elastic springback. Elastic springback = σ_fracture/E. This is always nonzero, so plastic strain is always less than total strain.
T1 — T5
"The lateral strain under tensile loading is positive."
FALSE. Under tensile axial loading, the specimen contracts laterally — lateral strain is negative. Poisson's ratio is defined as −ε_x/ε_z to make it a positive number.
T1 — T6
"The load-displacement curve is the same as the stress-strain curve."
FALSE. The load-displacement curve is geometry-dependent (a thicker bar supports more load). The stress-strain curve is geometry-independent — a material property. Always convert to stress and strain for material comparison.

Topic 2 & 3 — Deformation & Dislocations

T2 — T1
"φ in Schmid's Law is the angle between the tensile axis and the slip plane."
FALSE. φ is the angle between the tensile axis and the slip plane NORMAL. The slip plane itself is at angle (90° − φ) to the tensile axis. This is the most common Schmid exam error.
T2 — T2
"The maximum Schmid factor is 1.0."
FALSE. Maximum Schmid factor is 0.5, occurring when λ = φ = 45°. τ_max = σ/2, not σ. Exceeding 0.5 is geometrically impossible.
T2 — T3
"FCC metals have the same slip plane as BCC metals."
FALSE. FCC slip plane: {111}. BCC slip plane: {110}. However, both have slip direction ⟨110⟩ (FCC) and ⟨111⟩ (BCC) — these are different too. Don't mix them up.
T2 — T4
"HCP metals are as ductile as FCC metals."
FALSE. HCP has only 3 primary slip systems vs 12 for FCC and BCC. Fewer slip systems = less ability to accommodate arbitrary plastic deformation = lower ductility. Von Mises requires 5 independent systems for general ductility.
T3 — T1
"Edge dislocations can cross-slip."
FALSE. Only screw dislocations can cross-slip. An edge dislocation's line and Burgers vector together uniquely define a single glide plane — there is no alternative plane to cross-slip onto.
T3 — T2
"Screw dislocations can climb."
FALSE. Only edge dislocations can climb (non-conservative motion via vacancy diffusion). Screw dislocations have no extra half-plane and no mechanism for climb.
T3 — T3
"The Frank-Read critical stress uses the grain diameter as l."
FALSE. l is the length of the pinned dislocation segment — NOT the grain diameter. The segment is typically much shorter than the grain. τ_FR = Gb/l, not Gb/(grain diameter).
T3 — T4
"Work hardening decreases dislocation density."
FALSE. Work hardening increases dislocation density (from ~10¹⁰ to ~10¹⁶ m⁻²). More dislocations impede each other → higher yield stress. Recovery and recrystallisation decrease dislocation density.
T3 — T5
"Hall-Petch applies at any grain size."
FALSE. Hall-Petch breaks down below ~10 nm grain size. At nanoscale, grain boundary sliding dominates and the relationship inverts — finer grain actually becomes softer (inverse Hall-Petch).

Topic 4 — Strengthening & Annealing

T4 — T1
"Recovery is the main stage that restores ductility."
FALSE. Recrystallisation is the stage that restores ductility (large increase) and reduces strength (large decrease). Recovery barely changes mechanical properties — it mainly restores thermal and electrical conductivity.
T4 — T2
"Over-aging increases yield strength."
FALSE. Over-aging reduces yield strength. Precipitates coarsen (Ostwald ripening) → increased inter-particle spacing L → lower Orowan critical stress τ ≈ Gb/L. Peak-aged is the maximum; over-aging moves down from there.
T4 — T3
"Solid solution strengthening scales linearly with solute concentration."
FALSE. Solid solution strengthening scales as c^(1/2) — square root of concentration. Adding four times as much solute only doubles the strengthening effect.
T4 — T4
"More cold work raises the recrystallisation temperature."
FALSE. More cold work lowers the recrystallisation temperature. Greater stored energy (more dislocations) makes nucleation easier → recrystallisation starts at lower temperature.
T4 — T5
"Dispersion-strengthened materials (e.g. SAP) lose strength at high temperature like precipitation-hardened alloys."
FALSE. Dispersion-strengthened materials use thermally stable particles (Al₂O₃, SiC) that do not dissolve or coarsen at high temperature. Precipitation-hardened alloys lose strength at high T because precipitates dissolve into the matrix.

Topic 5 — Fracture

T5 — T1
"FCC metals show a ductile-to-brittle transition."
FALSE. FCC metals (Al, Cu, Ni, austenitic stainless steel) do NOT show DBT — they remain ductile at all temperatures. Only BCC and HCP metals show the transition. This is why austenitic SS is used for cryogenic applications.
T5 — T2
"Charpy impact energy can be used directly in fracture mechanics design calculations."
FALSE. Charpy data is qualitative — it gives a measure of energy absorption but is geometry-dependent and can't be directly used in design. Use K_IC (fracture toughness) for design calculations.
T5 — T3
"For a Griffith crack, a is always the half-length of the crack."
FALSE. Griffith convention: internal crack — a = half-length; edge crack — a = full length from free surface. Using the wrong convention introduces a factor of 2 error in a and √2 error in σ_f.
T5 — T4
"Increasing grain size lowers the ductile-to-brittle transition temperature."
FALSE. Larger grains raise the transition temperature (worse). This is opposite to the Hall-Petch effect on yield strength. Fine grain → lower T_t (better impact toughness).
T5 — T5
"Toughness (area under stress-strain curve) and fracture toughness K_IC are the same quantity."
FALSE. Toughness is energy per unit volume (J/m³) from a tensile test. Fracture toughness K_IC (MPa√m) describes resistance to crack propagation in the presence of a pre-existing flaw. They measure different aspects of material behaviour.

Topic 6 — Fatigue

T6 — T1
"Beachmarks are produced one per fatigue cycle."
FALSE. Striations are produced one per cycle. Beachmarks form from loading interruptions (machine shut-down, overload) and each beachmark contains thousands of striations.
T6 — T2
"Stage I fatigue cracks grow perpendicular to the applied stress."
FALSE. Stage I grows at 45° to the tensile axis along slip planes. Stage II grows perpendicular to the tensile axis. The angles are frequently swapped in exam distractors.
T6 — T3
"All metals have a true fatigue limit."
FALSE. Only ferritic steels have a true fatigue limit (S-N curve flattens). Aluminium, titanium, copper, and most non-ferrous alloys have no true limit — endurance is quoted at 10⁶–10⁷ cycles.
T6 — T4
"R = −1 means no loading."
FALSE. R = −1 means fully reversed loading — equal tension and compression, σ_m = 0. R = 0 is zero-to-max (pulsating tension). R = 1 would be constant load with no cycling.
T6 — T5
"Tensile mean stress improves fatigue life."
FALSE. Tensile mean stress reduces fatigue life (Goodman: σ_a decreases as σ_m increases). Compressive mean stress extends life — this is why shot peening works.
T6 — T6
"Paris Law applies to all stages of fatigue crack growth."
FALSE. Paris Law applies to Stage II only (stable propagation). It does not apply to Stage I (initiation along slip planes) or Stage III (fast fracture, K ≥ K_IC).
T6 — T7
"Shot peening works by hardening the surface."
FALSE. While peening does harden the surface slightly, the key mechanism is introducing a compressive residual stress layer. This compressive stress opposes crack opening under tensile fatigue loads, dramatically extending initiation life.

Topic 7 — Creep & SCC

T7 — T1
"Copper pipes in domestic heating systems are prone to creep."
FALSE. Copper T_m = 1356 K. At ~80°C (353 K): T/T_m = 0.26 — well below the 0.3–0.4 threshold. No significant creep. Lead (T_m = 600 K) creeps at room temperature (T/T_m = 0.5) — always use Kelvin.
T7 — T2
"The primary creep stage is the most important for engineering design."
FALSE. The secondary (steady-state) creep rate ε̇_s is the design parameter. Primary is transient and decelerating. Tertiary leads to fracture. Design lifetime is calculated from ε̇_s.
T7 — T3
"The activation energy Q_C equals R × gradient of the Arrhenius plot."
FALSE. Q_C = −R × gradient. The gradient of ln ε̇_s vs 1/T is negative (creep rate decreases as 1/T increases, i.e. as temperature decreases). The minus sign is critical and is the most common calculation error.
T7 — T4
"Nabarro-Herring creep dominates at engineering stresses."
FALSE. At engineering stresses, dislocation creep (n ≈ 5) dominates. Nabarro-Herring gives rates 6–7 orders of magnitude below observed at typical stresses. Diffusional creep only dominates at very high temperatures and very low stresses.
T7 — T5
"SCC only requires a tensile stress and a reactive environment."
FALSE. SCC requires all three simultaneously: tensile stress + reactive environment + susceptible material. Austenitic SS is susceptible to Cl⁻ SCC but titanium alloys may not be. Remove any one of the three → SCC stops.
T7 — T6
"More hydrogen dissolved in steel always makes it more susceptible to hydrogen embrittlement."
FALSE. HE is worst at intermediate temperatures near room temperature and at low strain rates. At very high temperatures, hydrogen diffuses away too fast. The rate of hydrogen transport relative to crack tip movement matters, not simply total hydrogen content.
T7 — T7
"Coble creep dominates over Nabarro-Herring at all temperatures."
FALSE. Coble (grain boundary diffusion) dominates at lower temperatures (0.4–0.7T_m) and finer grain sizes (ε̇ ∝ 1/d³). Nabarro-Herring (lattice diffusion) dominates at higher temperatures (>0.7T_m) and coarser grain sizes (ε̇ ∝ 1/d²).
